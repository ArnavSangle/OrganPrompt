\begin{abstract}
Organ segmentation in CT underpins radiotherapy planning and surgical guidance,
yet it rests on voxel-level labels traced by hand for every structure a model is
meant to recognize. Vision--language models suggest a structure could instead be
named, but they name well and locate poorly, because their objective compares
whole images with whole captions. Text-driven methods repair this with a second
network that draws the mask and so moves the decisive step beyond the prompt's
reach. We instead align each patch of a BiomedCLIP image encoder with the organ's
text embedding, against the ground-truth mask pooled to the $14\times14$ patch
grid, and read the segmentation straight off the forward pass with no decoder.
Across eleven organ classes on held-out TotalSegmentator volumes the method
reaches 0.651 mIoU, on par with a linear probe trained on the same coarse targets
without text (0.646). Because the organ is selected by the prompt alone, with
nothing between the prompt and the mask, the prompt's contribution can be
examined directly. Corrupting the positional wording collapses exactly the classes whose
identity depends on it, and one accidental two-word prompt error produced a
confident segmentation of the wrong organ. In an exploratory application to 180
involved vertebrae in 46 patients with spinal metastases, ranking organs by
distance from each vertebra placed an anatomically expected organ at mean rank
1.82 against 2.91 expected by chance ($p<0.0001$), with the residual errors
concentrated in two organ classes.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Knowing where an organ lies in a CT volume is a routine prerequisite for clinical
work: radiotherapy plans require the organs at risk around a target, surgical
planning needs the structures adjacent to a lesion, and follow-up imaging needs
organ volumes measured consistently over time. Automatic segmentation supplies
these and modern models do it well, with TotalSegmentator segmenting 104
anatomical structures at an agreement level comparable to an expert reader
\cite{wasserthal2023totalsegmentator}, so accuracy is not the open problem.

The problem is where that accuracy comes from. It rests on expert voxel-level
labels for every structure the model is meant to recognize, and those labels are
expensive in a way that better architectures do not relieve. Tracing an organ
boundary calls for a reader trained in cross-sectional anatomy, working on slices
where soft-tissue contrast is poor and neighboring structures share intensity, and
the work compounds quickly since every structure must be outlined on every slice of
every volume. Manual delineation of a single abdominal organ is estimated at 30--60
minutes per CT volume \cite{qu2023abdomenatlas}, and the authors of the large
public benchmarks attribute their limited organ coverage to exactly this labeling
cost \cite{ji2022amos, qu2023abdomenatlas}. Each dataset's label set is therefore
fixed when it is built: AMOS annotates 15 abdominal organs \cite{ji2022amos},
WORD 16 \cite{luo2022word} and CT-ORG six classes \cite{rister2020ctorg}, and a
structure outside that set is unavailable to anyone working from it without a new
round of expert tracing. In addition, at the ambiguous
boundaries where tracing is hardest, careful annotators disagree, so the ground
truth itself carries reader variability \cite{nikolov2021nsd}.

A different kind of supervision would remove that bottleneck. Descriptions of
anatomy already exist in quantity---in textbooks and in clinical reports---and
vision--language models learn visual concepts from such text alone
\cite{radford2021clip, zhai2023siglip}, with PMC-CLIP \cite{lin2023pmcclip} and
BiomedCLIP \cite{zhang2025biomedclip} carrying the approach into the medical
domain. If a written prompt could select a region as well as it selects a label,
segmentation would need no traced pixels at all.

The obstacle is what these models are trained to do. Their objective compares one
pooled image embedding against one pooled caption embedding, and nothing in it
asks any individual image patch to be discriminative on its own, so similarity
maps read from a pretrained model are close to useless as segmentations. The
standard repair, extracting spatial maps from a trained classifier after the fact
\cite{zhou2016cam, selvaraju2017gradcam}, does not apply either, because a
contrastively pretrained model makes no per-organ decision for such a method to
explain. Recent text-driven segmentation methods therefore hand mask formation to
a second network, which works and also means the component deciding the boundary
is no longer the prompt.

We supervise the property directly instead. During fine-tuning, each patch of the
image encoder is aligned with the organ's text embedding against the ground-truth
mask pooled to the $14\times14$ patch grid. This is spatial supervision derived
from dense annotation, only 256 times coarser, and we state it as such: the
method needs traced masks to train, although far less precise ones than a
pixel-level decoder would learn from. Patch-text similarity then becomes readable
as a spatial map, and
at inference the map comes straight off the forward pass, with no gradients, no
classifier, and no second-stage refiner. Nothing stands between the prompt and the
output, which is what lets us ask what the prompt is actually doing.

The method recovers most of the localization signal for morphologically
distinctive organs from these coarse targets, and degrades on small, low-contrast
structures that occupy only a few patches of the $14\times14$ grid. It does not
localize better than a linear probe given the same targets and the same frozen
features without any text, and the two are on par. We report that comparison
prominently because it clarifies what the text contributes. The accuracy comes
from the backbone and the coarse masks. The text lets the organ be chosen by a
written prompt and read off the forward pass with nothing in between, so the
prompt's contribution and its failures can be examined directly. A fixed classifier head does not allow this.
The frozen model is then carried onto a cohort of patients with spinal
metastases, a dataset and protocol it never saw in training, and asked, as an
exploratory analysis, which organ lies nearest each involved vertebra. Our
contributions are:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.3em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item A patch-level alignment objective that makes text-prompted localization
work from mask targets pooled to the patch grid, read out with no decoder, and an
equal-supervision comparison showing that it matches, but does not exceed, a
linear probe trained on the same targets without text
(\S\ref{sec:results:phase1}).
\item A prompt ablation establishing what the text does. Swapping the positional
wording collapses the classes whose identity depends on laterality and leaves
uniquely positioned organs untouched, so the prompt does real work and is not a
class index in disguise (\S\ref{sec:results:ablation}).
\item A failure mode with safety implications; an accidental two-word error in one
of eleven prompts produced a clean, confident segmentation of the wrong organ
(\S\ref{sec:results:ablation}).
\item An exploratory downstream use of the frozen model on an unseen dataset and
protocol, ranking organs by distance from each involved vertebra, with the
residual failure traced to two of the eleven organ classes
(\S\ref{sec:results:index}).
\item Two negative results with identified mechanisms. We adopted neither
pre-registered calibration variant, and in each case we can say why
(\S\ref{sec:results:negatives}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

The approach this work builds on begins with general-purpose vision--language
pretraining. CLIP showed that predicting which caption belongs to which image, over 400M
web-collected pairs, gives representations strong enough for zero-shot transfer
across more than 30 benchmarks \cite{radford2021clip}, and SigLIP replaced the
softmax-normalized objective with a pairwise sigmoid loss that decouples the loss
from batch size \cite{zhai2023siglip}. Later analyzes attribute much of the
progress to data curation \cite{xu2024metaclip, gadre2023datacomp}, which
motivates the biomedical variants, where domain-matched data is scarce: PMC-CLIP
collects 1.6M image--caption pairs from PubMed Central \cite{lin2023pmcclip}, and
BiomedCLIP scales to 15M figure--caption pairs from 4.4M articles with a
PubMedBERT encoder, a 30k domain vocabulary and a 256-token context
\cite{zhang2025biomedclip}. The longer context is what makes multi-sentence
anatomical prompts expressible, so we build on BiomedCLIP. None of these models
grounds text spatially, because the objective constrains pooled representations
only, so no gradient asks a patch token to align with text on its own, and
self-attention in the deeper blocks mixes globally, leaving a late-layer patch
token reflecting image-wide context rather than the evidence at its own location.

That pooling limitation has been attacked before, and three lines of work address
it. The first extracts dense correspondence from
frozen weights: MaskCLIP reads CLIP's value projection instead of the pooled
output \cite{zhou2022maskclip}, later work pushes that recovery further for weakly
supervised segmentation \cite{yang2025clipdense}, a related line asks why raw CLIP
attention localizes poorly \cite{li2024clipexplain}, and self-supervised ViTs show
similar structure with no text at all \cite{caron2021dino}. We agree about where
the signal lives but train the property in, because our downstream analysis needs
organ maps that vanish where the organ is absent, which is a calibration property.
The second line adapts the model with a task head, as in DenseCLIP
\cite{rao2022denseclip} and CLIPSeg \cite{lueddecke2022clipseg}; both give better
boundaries than we do, and both put a trained decoder between prompt and mask. The
third builds locality in during pretraining, and GLoRIA is the closest medical
antecedent, matching image sub-regions to words in radiology reports for
label-efficient recognition \cite{huang2021gloria}; we instead supervise the 196
patch tokens against a downsampled mask, so the similarity map is itself the
segmentation.

Within text-driven medical segmentation itself, MedCLIP-SAMv2 is the closest
relative. It shares the BiomedCLIP backbone and the
text-driven premise \cite{koleilat2025medclipsamv2, koleilat2024medclipsam}, but
the mask comes from elsewhere: vision--language saliency prompts SAM
\cite{kirillov2023sam}, which draws the final boundary, following MedSAM
\cite{ma2024medsam}. Handing mask formation to a second model is the stronger
engineering choice and gives better boundaries, but it also changes what the
pipeline can be asked, since a refiner can repair a bad localization or corrupt a
good one and a failure can then no longer be attributed to the text. Our caption
ablation (\S\ref{sec:results:ablation}) is interpretable only because nothing sits
between the prompt and the mask.

That lineage and ours are often grouped as weakly supervised, and the label is
loose enough to mislead, so we state our supervision exactly
(Table~\ref{tab:supervision}). OrganPrompt does not learn from image-level labels.
It trains on each ground-truth mask average-pooled to the $14\times14$ patch grid
and binarized, 196 labels per slice in place of 50{,}176 pixels. This is spatial supervision derived from dense annotation at 1/256 of its resolution. At
inference it needs only the image and the prompt, but so does any trained
segmentation model, so that property is not a saving in annotation and we do not
claim it as one.

The tiers frame how coarse spatial supervision can be and still localize.
Fully supervised architectures, from U-Net \cite{ronneberger2015unet} and
TransUNet \cite{chen2021transunet} to recent attention-augmented U-Nets
\cite{xiao2025resunet} and edge--context fusion networks \cite{xiao2025brainseg},
learn from full-resolution masks and set the upper reference. Point- and
scribble-supervised methods receive a clicked pixel or a stroke inside each
structure \cite{jiang2024probabilistic}, usually on AMOS \cite{ji2022amos} or WORD
\cite{luo2022word}, so they are told roughly where each structure is but not its
extent. Image-level labels answer ``where'' not at all: CAM-based lesion
segmentation in PET-CT reports a median Dice of 0.47 against 0.72 for a fully
supervised U-Net on the same task \cite{fruh2021petct}. The localization technique
native to that tier is the CAM family \cite{zhou2016cam, selvaraju2017gradcam,
chattopadhay2018gradcampp}, and every member of it explains where a trained
classifier looked, whereas a contrastively pretrained model outputs a similarity
and not a class logit, so gradient-based localization recovers almost nothing
from it. That is the 0.003 row of Table~\ref{tab:progression}.

Because our supervision sits between the full and the point tiers, cross-tier
comparisons would flatter it. The comparison that isolates what the text adds is
within our own tier: a model given the same coarse targets and the same frozen
features but no text. We therefore report a linear probe trained on exactly our
patch targets (\S\ref{sec:results:phase1}). Beyond that, the work differs from
its neighbors in two ways: the similarity map is read directly with no refiner,
which preserves attribution to the prompt, and the frozen model is carried into a
downstream question on a protocol it never saw. We tested the idea on the
OrganAMNIST subset of MedMNIST v2 \cite{yang2023medmnist} before moving to
full-resolution CT.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Backbone and patch-level objective}
\label{sec:methods:patchloss}

We fine-tune BioMedCLIP \cite{zhang2025biomedclip}, a ViT-B/16 visual encoder
\cite{dosovitskiy2021vit} with a PubMedBERT text encoder \cite{gu2021pubmedbert},
195M parameters in total. We build on a CLIP-family model \cite{radford2021clip}
for one reason: the category is written in natural language at inference rather
than enumerated during training, so nothing fixes the label set in advance.
Fine-tuning updates only the upper blocks of both encoders and the projection and
logit parameters, about 57M of 195M, with AdamW (weight decay 0.01), 200 warmup
steps then cosine annealing, batch size 32 and $\lambda=0.5$ throughout. The peak
learning rate was $10^{-4}$ for the CLS-only stage and the final run and
$3\times10^{-5}$ for the intermediate patch-loss runs, as recorded in each saved
optimizer state, and Table~\ref{tab:progression} lists the learning rate and epochs of
every stage. The layer names and training configuration are in the repository.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.88\textwidth]{pipeline.png}
\caption{Method overview. \textbf{(A)} The patch-level training objective
(\S\ref{sec:methods:patchloss}), in which the two grids flanking the loss are the
arrays the per-patch BCE compares. \textbf{(B)} Inference
(\S\ref{sec:methods:inference}), a single forward pass with no gradients,
classifier or mask refiner, followed by the downstream step: competitive

A shows a TotalSegmentator liver slice, B a Spine-Mets slice (case 10355, level

The training objective is the central component of the method and the largest
single contributor to performance (Table~\ref{tab:progression});
Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}A shows it. Writing $e_p$ for the normalized embedding of
patch $p$, $t$ for the normalized organ text embedding, $s$ for the learned logit
scale and $y_p\in\{0,1\}$ for the patch label read off the mask pooled to the
$14\times14$ grid and binarized at 0.5,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{patch}}
  = \frac{1}{196}\sum_{p=1}^{196}
    \mathrm{BCE}\!\left(s \cdot \langle e_p, t\rangle,\; y_p\right),
\qquad
\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{SigLIP}} + \lambda\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{patch}},
\end{equation}
with $\lambda = 0.5$. Patches inside the organ are pulled toward the organ's text
embedding and patches outside are pushed away, and that separation is what makes
per-patch similarity readable as a spatial map: in the example of
Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}A, mean similarity to the liver prompt is $+0.087$
inside the pooled mask against $-0.182$ outside. ViT-B/16 geometry at
$224\times224$ input fixes patch supervision at $14\times14$, and that same liver
fills 78 of the 196 cells while a thin organ occupies only a few, so boundary
precision is coarse by construction and the HD95 of 19.6\,px reported in
\S\ref{sec:results:phase1} mostly reflects the upsampled patch grid. This is an
architectural property of the backbone and not an unturned knob.

\subsection{Inference, calibration and competitive assignment}
\label{sec:methods:inference}

Inference (Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}B) is a single forward pass: four blocks are
fused instead of one, no mask is involved, and no gradient is computed. The map
for organ $c$ is the raw cosine similarity $\langle e_p, t_c\rangle$ at each of
the 196 patches, upsampled to $224\times224$, and averaging blocks 8--11 combines
the semantic response of the last two blocks with the finer texture and edge
response of blocks 8--9. Resolution therefore makes a round trip: a slice is
resized from its native in-plane grid to $224\times224$ before encoding, readout
happens on the $14\times14$ grid, and the map is returned to the native grid
before any mask is formed or any distance measured, so no spatial quantity is
computed at the resized scale. An optional hard-negative variant subtracts the strongest
competing response over a set of negative prompts; it is tested and not adopted in
\S\ref{sec:results:negatives}, so it is off by default.

Two conventional choices turned out to be harmful, and removing both gave $+0.059$
mIoU with no retraining. Per-image min--max normalization forces every slice to
span the full dynamic range whether or not the organ is present, destroying the
information that separates ``this organ is here'' from ``this organ is absent from
this slice.'' Our maps are therefore raw cosine similarities, bounded in $[-1,1]$
and often negative across a whole slice. Organs win relative to each other, not
against an absolute cutoff, so every visualization in this paper thresholds at a
percentile of the slice's own distribution and scales its colour ramp to its own
range, which makes brightness relative and never an absolute confidence.
Class-conditional
negative subtraction, the second choice, cut more foreground signal than
background. In place of both, per-class thresholds are set by sweeping IoU-optimal
cutoffs, the only fitted quantity in the Phase~1 pipeline. In the revised
evaluation the sweep runs on the validation split and the thresholds are then
fixed for the test split. The submitted version swept them on the test split
itself, and we report both so the two can be compared
(\S\ref{sec:results:phase1}).

Whole volumes expose a further problem that per-slice classification hides.
Thresholding each organ's map independently means every organ always receives a
mask: a bladder prompt run over a chest CT still has a global maximum somewhere,
so it claims the most bladder-like thoracic tissue and reports itself present, and
absence is not expressible. Neither component size nor peak similarity separates
these phantoms from correct detections, since a correct spleen appeared at the 0th
size percentile while a wrong bladder sat at the 65th, so no per-organ gate can
remove them. Competition can, because
the organs then contest the same voxels: each voxel goes to at most one organ, the
one with the strongest calibrated response there, and an organ whose own best
voxel is taken by a stronger organ is reported absent.

The contest needs calibration, since raw similarity is not comparable across
prompts and lungs peak near 0.18 against 0.06 for femurs, so an uncalibrated
argmax would give nearly every voxel to the lungs. The default \emph{scale} mode
divides each organ's map by that organ's typical peak response, putting all organs
on a common ``fraction of the response this organ ever gives'' scale, while a
\emph{z-score} mode computes $(\text{raw}-\mu_c)/\sigma_c$ per organ and also
corrects baseline offset; the z-score mode is the variant evaluated and not
adopted in \S\ref{sec:results:negatives}. Bilateral pairs need one further rule,
because letting \org{kidney-left} and \org{kidney-right} bid against each other
reproduced the same bug one level down, with an early measurement showing 37\% of
pairs splitting this way. Presence is therefore decided for the pair as a group
and the group's territory divided at the image midline, following the convention
the prompts state consistently, that a patient-left organ ``appears on the RIGHT
side of the image''. A calibrated value must also exceed a floor of 0.6, or every
voxel in the body would be assigned to something.

\subsection{Data and evaluation protocol}
\label{sec:methods:data}

Training and evaluation use TotalSegmentator v2.0.1
\cite{wasserthal2023totalsegdataset}: 1{,}228 thoracic and abdominal CT volumes
with per-voxel labels. We use eleven classes, forming the two lungs by merging the
released per-lobe labels. Of the 1{,}228 volumes, 1{,}166 contain at least one of
the eleven classes and are split 80/10/10 at the volume level with a fixed seed
(42): 931 training, 115 validation and 120 test volumes. Within a volume the top
$K=5$ slices per organ are indexed, giving 35{,}206 training, 4{,}561 validation
and 4{,}506 test slices (per-class counts in Appendix~\ref{app:split}). All slices
of a volume fall in the same split. The release identifies examinations, not
patients, and carries no patient identifier, so if one patient contributed more
than one examination those examinations could fall in different splits, and the released metadata does not let us rule this out. We did not use the split
suggested in the release metadata.

Preprocessing applies an organ-dependent HU window ($[-1000,-200]$ for lungs,
$[-200,1000]$ for femurs, $[-150,250]$ otherwise), rescales to $[0,1]$ and resizes
to $224\times224$. Six of the eleven classes are defined by laterality and the
prompts carry it explicitly, so a horizontal flip would silently invert the label;
no rotation or flip augmentation is used.

We report 2D per-slice IoU and Dice on organ-present slices, together with HD95,
sensitivity, precision, relative volume error and normalized surface Dice
\cite{nikolov2021nsd} at $\tau=3$\,px. This protocol is more forgiving than 3D
volumetric scoring, because no empty or partial-boundary slices pull the average
down, and every cross-paper comparison below inherits that caveat. Checkpoints are
ranked by the mask-metric suite and not by validation loss, which is the SigLIP
CLS-level objective and can improve while the patch maps get worse. In the
submitted version that ranking was computed on the test split, so the reported
checkpoint (epoch 11 of the final run) was selected on test data. The revision
re-selects on the validation split and reports the result
(\S\ref{sec:results:phase1}). Our
fully supervised comparator throughout is the nnU-Net \cite{isensee2021nnunet}
released with TotalSegmentator, trained on 1{,}204 routine clinical examinations
covering 104 structures at a mean Dice of 0.943
\cite{wasserthal2023totalsegmentator}.

\subsection{Ablation design}
\label{sec:methods:captions}

To separate the prompt's contribution from the visual encoder's, we fine-tuned the Patch-tuned-III model of Table~\ref{tab:progression} and re-evaluated it under five prompt formulations (Table~\ref{tab:captiondefs}), making sure to vary only the text embedding and retraining nothing. The five prompt formulations are as follows: class name only (L1), a generic clinical caption (L2), position language with the organ name
removed (L3), the full multi-sentence anatomical description used in training (L4), and an adversarial variant (L5). All five sets are in the repository.
Each formulation is scored on the same 300 test slices (22--35 per class), with
the per-class threshold re-swept for each formulation on those slices. Every level
is therefore scored at its own best threshold, so the drops reported for L3 and L5 are conservative.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Prompt formulations used in the ablation.}
\label{tab:captiondefs}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
Level & Prompt formulation \\
\midrule
L1 & Class name only \\
L2 & Generic clinical caption \\
L3 & Position only (organ name removed) \\
L4 & Full anatomical description (used in training) \\
L5 & Adversarial (L4 with position words swapped) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

L5 carries most of the weight of the ablation, and it asks one question: does the
model read the spatial content of the prompt, or only the organ name? Every L4
prompt locates its organ along exactly two image axes, horizontal (the laterality
wording ``appears on the RIGHT side of the image'') and vertical (``top half'',
``upper'', ``bottom''), because an axial slice has no third in-plane axis to
describe. L5 therefore exchanges the terms of both axes, left$\leftrightarrow$right,
upper$\leftrightarrow$lower and top$\leftrightarrow$bottom, in every case form,
leaving word count and clause structure unchanged. Because the substitution also
applies to the organ name, it has two different effects. For the six bilateral
classes it turns the prompt into a coherent description of the contralateral organ
(the \org{femur-left} prompt becomes ``the right femur \ldots\ on the LEFT side of
the image''), so a collapse means the model followed the text to the other side.
For the five unpaired classes the name is unchanged and only the position words
flip, so the prompt contradicts the image. The substitution matched substrings,
and it also altered three words that merely contain a swapped term, in two of the
eleven prompts: ``bright'' and ``lower-density'' in \org{femur-left} and
``brighter'' in \org{kidney-left}. We report the prompts as run, in the
repository, and check in \S\ref{sec:results:ablation} that these corruptions do
not drive the result. Other adversarial edits are possible and untested here:
swapping organ names while keeping positions, negating clauses, shuffling
sentence order, or replacing anatomy with plausible but wrong landmarks. L5 tests
only the spatial channel, and a class that survives it was relying on something
other than position.

One caveat belongs with the method. Our L3 prompt for \org{lung-right} was
generated incorrectly and names the left lung and the left half of the image. We
found this during analysis and did not design it, so \S\ref{sec:results:ablation}
reports it as a natural experiment and not as a controlled manipulation.

\subsection{Downstream analysis: organ proximity at involved vertebrae}
\label{sec:methods:index}

The downstream analysis reuses the Phase~1 model, frozen and unmodified, on
Spine-Mets-CT-SEG \cite{pieper2024spinemets}: 55 patients of 3D DICOM CT with
per-vertebra SEG masks. Involvement per vertebral level comes from the
collection's clinical spreadsheet and not from the SEG file, so the reference
region for each lesion is the whole involved vertebra, which we call the
\emph{involved-vertebra mask}. The collection does not delineate tumor extent,
and every distance below is measured from the vertebra, not from the lesion
inside it. Figure~\ref{fig:flow} gives the patient flow: nine patients have no
lesion listed and serve only for calibration, and the other 46 carry 184 listed
involved levels, of which 180 keep voxels after the slice stride of two used at
inference and are evaluated.

SEG frames are placed on the CT grid through the DICOM geometry of both series.
Each frame's position and orientation vectors map its pixels into patient
coordinates, the CT's own vectors map them back into its row--column grid, and
frames are matched to CT slices by through-plane position within 2\,mm. In all 55
patients the SEG stores its column direction opposite to the CT's, $(0,-1,0)$
against $(0,1,0)$. An earlier version of this analysis placed SEG frames
index-for-index and so mirrored every vertebra from front to back. All downstream
numbers in this paper are recomputed with the corrected placement, and the same
placement feeds the distance computation and every figure. As a check, the mean CT
value inside the vertebra mask is bone-like ($>150$\,HU) for 780 of 782 labeled
levels (median 321\,HU). The two exceptions are T1 masks that, as released,
continue 7 and 12 slices above the vertebral body onto air. They are left as released, and one of them is an evaluated lesion. Figure~\ref{fig:alignment}
shows the two placements on the cases of Figure~\ref{fig:qualitative}.

OrganPrompt is 2D, so each axial slice is preprocessed as in training and passed
through the patch-similarity engine, and the per-slice maps are stacked and
resampled to the native in-plane resolution, giving a 3D organ volume that
competitive assignment reduces to mutually exclusive 3D masks. Vertebra voxels are
removed from every organ mask, since an organ predicted inside a vertebra is an
artifact. For an involved-vertebra mask $V$ and an organ mask $O$ the score is the
minimum physical distance
\begin{equation}
d_{\text{mm}}(V,O) = \min_{v\in V,\;o\in O}\;\bigl\lVert \Delta\,(v-o)\bigr\rVert_2,
\end{equation}
where $\Delta$ is the diagonal matrix of voxel spacings, computed with a KD-tree
over organ voxel coordinates. Organs are ranked by ascending $d_{\text{mm}}$, and
an organ that competitive assignment reports absent is unranked. The submitted
version described this as a blend $\alpha\,|V\cap O|/|V| + \beta/(1+d_{\text{mm}})$. Because the vertebra voxels are removed from $O$, the overlap term is identically
zero, the blend orders organs exactly as $d_{\text{mm}}$ does, and we now state the
distance ranking directly. Before trusting the organ maps on this cohort we
checked them on CT-ORG \cite{rister2020ctorg}, an independent organ-labeled CT
collection, to confirm they transfer to a protocol the model never saw.

The ranking answers a localization question and not a clinical one. Because $V$ is
the whole vertebra, $d_{\text{mm}}$ measures how close an organ lies to the involved
vertebra, not to the tumor, and it says nothing about invasion: a lesion confined
to the posterior elements and an anterior lesion abutting the aorta share the same
$V$. What the analysis tests is whether the model's nearest organ at each involved
level agrees with the organs anatomy places there, that is, whether frozen organ
maps transfer to an unseen protocol well enough to support a proximity
measurement. A clinically meaningful proximity index would need lesion-level
delineation, which this collection does not provide, and we present the
downstream results as exploratory evidence of transfer, not as a validated index.

Validating per patient caps out at 20 evaluation points, the patients with exactly
one involved level. Scoring per lesion raises this to 180 lesions across 46
patients at no additional inference cost, since a patient's organ masks are scored
against every lesion while they are resident. The quantity scored is the rank of
the nearest anatomically expected organ in the model's distance-ordered list.
Which organs are plausible at each vertebral level is fixed in a lookup table
built from anatomical references \cite{oh2009retrodiaphragmatic}, with the full
source list, the quoted passages and the question put to each source given in
Appendix~\ref{app:anatomy} and recorded in the repository.

Scoring a rank against that lookup table only means something once chance is
defined precisely, and chance is not one constant here. The expected-organ set size $k$ varies by level,
so the chance mean rank for a lesion is the expectation of the minimum of $k$ draws
without replacement from $n=11$ organs, $\mathbb{E}[\text{rank}] = (n+1)/(k+1)$,
and we report the mean of that quantity over lesions. Correcting the
expected-organ table therefore changes chance as well as the observed statistic,
which is why our anatomy fix moves chance from 3.01 to 2.91.

Uncertainty is estimated by bootstrapping over patients, not lesions
\cite{efron1994bootstrap}, with 5{,}000 resamples of patients drawn with
replacement and all of a drawn patient's lesions carried with it, because lesions
within a patient share a scan, a set of organ masks and that patient's anatomy.
Treating 180 lesions as 180 independent trials would tighten the interval by
roughly the square root of the mean lesions per patient, about $2\times$ here,
which is larger than the effect of any change we made to the method. The
significance test is a permutation test with 20{,}000 permutations, whose null
draws a fresh random ranking for each lesion using that lesion's own $k$.

Both calibration variants in \S\ref{sec:results:negatives} were specified in full
before running, including the z-score coefficient $k=2$, and are reported as run.
Neither was retuned afterwards, and we did not try a smaller $k$. We also state
our exposure, since it bounds how much any single comparison should be trusted:
the 180-lesion set has absorbed the calibration fit, the bilateral-grouping
change, the move from per-patient to per-lesion scoring, and the two variants.

Calibration constants are cross-fitted at the patient level. The 46 evaluated
patients are split into five folds with a seed fixed in advance, and each
patient is scored with constants fitted on the other four folds together with the
nine lesion-free patients, who are never evaluated (Figure~\ref{fig:flow}). No
patient is therefore calibrated on its own scan. This replaces two earlier fits.
The very first fitted the constants on the 20 single-level evaluation patients
themselves. The submitted version refitted them on the other 35 patients, a set that excluded the 20 but included 26 of the 46 patients later evaluated per lesion,
so its per-lesion numbers still contained a partial leak. The cross-fit removes it.

The pipeline was developed on this cohort, and we had no independent cohort with
vertebral-level involvement labels, so the downstream results are exploratory. The
configuration used for every downstream number in this revision, including the
geometry correction, the cross-fit and its seed, was fixed and recorded before any
of its results were seen, and we report it as run.

\subsection{Text-prompted organ localization}
\label{sec:results:phase1}

The final model, the Final checkpoint of Table~\ref{tab:progression} at epoch 11,
reaches mean IoU 0.651 and mean Dice 0.756 on the TotalSegmentator test split
across 11 classes, with per-class thresholds swept on the validation split and
then fixed, and this is the figure we treat as primary. Sweeping the thresholds on the

mIoU. Re-selecting over all 20 epochs of the final run by validation mIoU chooses
epoch 8 (0.648 against 0.647 for epoch 11), whose test mIoU is 0.653 against
0.651. The difference is below the 0.01 we set in advance as material, so epoch 11
is kept and every downstream result uses it.

\subsection{What the prompt contributes}
\label{sec:results:ablation}

A text-conditioned localizer invites an obvious objection: the text may be a
passenger, with the visual encoder doing the work and the prompt acting as a class
index. Table~\ref{tab:captions} and Figure~\ref{fig:captionheat} test that, with
the five formulations restated beneath Table~\ref{tab:captions} so the levels can
be read without turning back to Table~\ref{tab:captiondefs}.

\textbf{(1) Class names carry most of the signal.} L1 reaches 96.9\% of L4's
mIoU, so a single word recovers nearly everything a multi-sentence description
provides. Anatomical detail does not buy the remaining 3\% either: a one-line
generic caption (L2) matches the full description (L4) to within 0.0006 on both
metrics, and the two split them. What L1 lacks is supplied by having any sentence
context at all, not by the anatomy written into it.

\textbf{(2) Position language refines rather than substitutes.} L3 keeps position
cues and removes organ identity, and scores below L1 (0.561 vs.\ 0.607), so the
model needs to know what to look for before where becomes useful.

The aggregate numbers hide which classes move, and the per-class view in
Figure~\ref{fig:captionheat} is where the next two findings come from.

\textbf{(3) The text is not a passenger.} Under L5, the classes that depend on
left/right disambiguation collapse (kidney-left $-63\%$, lung-left $-57\%$, femurs
$-56\%$), while organs with unique positions are unaffected (liver, spleen, heart,
bladder). The model uses the positional content, and corrupting it degrades the
classes for which it matters. The three substring corruptions noted in
\S\ref{sec:methods:captions} do not explain this: \org{femur-right}, whose L5
prompt is uncorrupted, falls as far as \org{femur-left} ($-57\%$ against
$-56\%$), and \org{kidney-right}, also uncorrupted, still falls $47\%$.

\textbf{(3a) Robustness tracks position-invariant anatomical content.} The two
lungs get the same L5 transformation and respond differently: lung-left falls
$0.83\to0.36$ ($-57\%$), lung-right only $0.78\to0.63$ ($-19\%$). Lung-left's
distinguishing clause, \emph{``slightly smaller than the right due to the heart
pushing into the left hemithorax''}, inverts cleanly and ends up describing the
other lung, whereas lung-right's prompt contains a clause the swap cannot corrupt:
\emph{``Three lobes are present, divided by fissures visible as thin lines.''} The
right lung has three lobes and the left has two, so that sentence still points at
the right lung after the position words are flipped, and morphological cues that
survive a position swap therefore harden a bilateral class against the failure L5
probes.

\textbf{(4) Pancreas is data-limited, not prompt-limited.} Pancreas IoU stays at

small, low-contrast structures.

The L3 level supplied one further result, and unlike the four findings above we
did not design it. Its auto-generated
prompt for \org{lung-right} was wrong in both content words. It reads \emph{``The
left lung occupies the left half of the image''} where the L4 convention requires
\emph{``The right lung \ldots{} the left half.''} Organ name and position are both
wrong and mutually consistent, so the sentence is a valid description of the left
lung. The model did not become confused or degrade gracefully. It followed the
prompt and segmented the left lung. IoU on \org{lung-right} at L3 fell to 0.23,
while \org{lung-left} at L3 held at 0.829, essentially its 0.828 at L4, so the
level itself was harmless and the cause is the two wrong words. A two-word error
in one of eleven prompts cost 55 IoU points on the affected class, and the failure
was directional and not degradative: a wrong but coherent prompt gives a confident
segmentation of the wrong structure. In a clinical setting that is more dangerous
than low confidence, because nothing in the output signals it.

\subsection{Downstream analysis: organ ranking at involved vertebrae (exploratory)}
\label{sec:results:index}

The same frozen model is next asked a different question: at each involved
vertebra, which organ lies nearest? Scoring one point per involved vertebra gives
180 points across 46 patients, 178 of them rankable. The mean rank of the first
anatomically expected organ is 1.82, 95\% CI $[1.65, 2.02]$ by patient-clustered
bootstrap, against a chance mean rank of 2.91. No permutation out of 20{,}000
reached the observed value ($p<0.0001$), and chance lies outside the confidence

one correction to that key changes the numbers above, so we report it here. Our
expected-organ table originally dropped the lungs after T10. Anatomical sources

\cite{oh2009retrodiaphragmatic}, so the table was wrong and we corrected it on

question put to each set out in Appendix~\ref{app:anatomy}. We decided the
correction from the literature before applying it and did not check which version
improved the metric. Scored on the revised rankings, it moves mean rank from 1.99
to 1.82, top-1 from 0.539 to 0.594 and anatomically unexpected top-1 detections
from 42 to 35, and chance from 3.01 to 2.91, because the expected sets at T11 and
T12 grow. Band membership in Figure~\ref{fig:perlevel} is derived from each level's
own observed-versus-chance comparison and not from a hardcoded list of levels.

at L5, the one level where a femur is expected. The corrected placement also

against 26 in the submitted analysis, and both lungs gain, as expected once the
vertebrae sit between the lungs instead of in the mediastinum.

\subsection{Analysis of failure cases}
\label{sec:results:negatives}

The phantom pattern has more than one possible explanation, so we tested two. Both
configurations were fixed in advance for the submitted version, and both are
rerun here unchanged on the corrected placement and calibration.

The simplest explanation is that phantom patients' scans do not extend far enough
caudally to contain the true organ, so a wrong organ wins by default. A
header-only scan-coverage measurement rejects it. The 13 patients with a phantom
have more caudal coverage than the 33 without, median 132\,mm against 82.5\,mm
below the last segmented vertebra, and 11 of the 13 reach L5.

Field of view is not the explanation, so we turned to the prompts themselves. A
text-encoder-only diagnostic showed that the \org{femur} prompt embeds almost
identically to an undefined \org{humerus} prompt, closer than to any of femur's
own defined classmates, and that \org{bladder}'s nearest neighbor is an undefined
\org{aorta} prompt. That motivated adding rib, humerus and aorta as negative
prompts. Table~\ref{tab:variants} reports that variant and a z-score calibration
floor. We adopted neither.

Global hard negatives traded one confusion for another: femur pair splitting fell
from 38 to 4, but lung pairs, which never split in the baseline, split 45 times,
kidney splits rose from 10 to 26, unexpected top-1 detections rose from 35 to 58,
and top-1 and top-3 both fell. The mechanism is specific: the rib negative is
subtracted from every organ's score and not only from femur's, so it bleeds into
the lungs at the rib--lung boundary, where lung tissue legitimately abuts rib. The
z-score floor removed bilateral pair splitting entirely, 48 to 0, but more than
doubled unexpected top-1 detections, 35 to 90, and 54 of those 90 win at 1\,mm or
less (median 0.9\,mm, against 28\,mm for the baseline's). That is consistent with a
floor that is too permissive: most organs keep a sliver of territory almost
everywhere instead of vanishing where the organ is absent, and a sliver beside the
vertebra is enough to win a proximity contest.

One caution applies to reading Table~\ref{tab:variants}. Both variants leave more
vertebrae unrankable than the baseline, 170 and 165 against 178, so their mean
ranks average over a smaller and easier subset, which flatters that column. Top-1
and top-3 are computed over all 180 and are the cleaner comparison, and both
variants fall on those, which agrees with the unexpected-detection counts.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Three results carry most of the weight below. Given the same coarse spatial
supervision, aligning patches to text localizes as well as a linear readout of
the same frozen features and no better, so the text matters for how the organ is
selected and examined, not for accuracy. Within the prompt, the class name does
most of the work, recovering 96.9\% of the full description's mIoU, while
position language matters for the classes it disambiguates. Downstream, the
organ ranking fails through two specific classes, not through a hard
anatomical region. All three are bounded by a $14\times14$ patch grid and by
single-cohort, exploratory evaluation of the downstream analysis.

\subsection{What the text adds, and where it stops}

The equal-supervision comparison settles a question the development history in
Table~\ref{tab:progression} could not. That history credits the patch objective
with the largest single gain, 0.063 to 0.307 mIoU, and within the method that is
right: it is what turns patch--text similarity into a spatial map. But a linear
layer on the pretrained features, trained on the same $14\times14$ targets with no
text at all, reaches 0.646 against our 0.651, and shows the same ordering by organ
size and contrast. The localization accuracy is therefore a property of the
backbone and the supervision. What the text alignment adds is that the map is
addressed by a written prompt and read off with nothing in between, and two
consequences of that follow.

The first is that the prompt's contribution can be measured. A classifier head never allows that. Prompt elaboration bought nothing measurable: the class name
alone recovers 96.9\% of the full description's mIoU, and position language
without identity scores below identity alone (0.561 vs.\ 0.607), so anyone
extending this approach should start with class names and one-line captions.
Position language does work where it is needed, since the classes that depend on
laterality collapse when it is swapped and the rest do not. The pancreas states
the ceiling most clearly, with IoU flat at 0.213--0.218 across all five
formulations while the kidneys (0.45--0.49 IoU) collapse 47--63\% under the swap,
so prompt-limited and data-limited classes call for different fixes: better
laterality cues for the kidneys, a finer patch grid or a different backbone for the
pancreas.

The second is that failures are attributable to the text. The accidental L3 error,
naming the wrong organ and the wrong side at once, produced a clean, confident
segmentation of the left lung; failures are directional rather than degradative,
so prompt validation belongs in any deployed system built this way. The same
finding suggests a mitigation: \org{lung-right} survived the swap far better than
\org{lung-left} ($-19.5\%$ vs.\ $-56.8\%$) because its prompt contains a
laterality-independent clause, ``three lobes are present'', so hardening bilateral
prompts with position-invariant morphological cues is a controllable,
no-retraining fix. Calibration turned out to matter for the same reason the
readout does. Removing per-image min--max normalization added $+0.059$ mIoU and
halved HD95 with no retraining, because it destroyed exactly the signal that
separates organ-here from organ-absent, which the downstream analysis needs. We
do not claim the best boundaries: DenseCLIP \cite{rao2022denseclip}, CLIPSeg
\cite{lueddecke2022clipseg} and MedCLIP-SAMv2 \cite{koleilat2025medclipsamv2,
kirillov2023sam} (our closest methodological relative, at 78.21 mean DSC
zero-shot and 82.11 with pseudo-labels) all produce cleaner edges than an
upsampled $14\times14$ grid, and our own probe has the lower HD95. What we get in
exchange is attribution: a 55-point IoU drop tracing to two words is attributable
only because no intermediate component could have absorbed or amplified the error.

\subsection{What the downstream failures are made of}

The failures of the downstream ranking have a finer structure than a uniform
thoracolumbar failure suggests. T12 and L5 sit near chance, not far below
it (misses of 0.20 and 0.08), T12 against the study's most stringent bar (seven
expected organs, chance 1.50). L1 is a small miss ($+0.12$), and L2 is the one
clear failure ($+0.60$ against a lenient chance of 3.00). That failure traces to
two specific classes, not to a hard region. \org{bladder} is the top-ranked
organ at 5 of 10 L2 vertebrae, where it is nowhere near, and at L5, the one level
where femurs are expected, neither femur class ever ranks first, while the femur
classes win 21 times elsewhere and are never correct. This matches a text-only
diagnostic showing the \org{femur} prompt embeds almost identically to an
undefined \org{humerus} prompt, and Figure~\ref{fig:qualitative} shows a femur
mask sitting on rib and lung base beside a thoracic vertebra. We ran two
pre-registered variants aimed at this behavior and adopted neither. Global hard
negatives cut femur pair splitting from 38 to 4 but bled into the lungs (0 to 45
splits) and raised unexpected top-1 detections from 35 to 58, because the negative
is subtracted from every organ's score and not only from the classes it targets.
The implied fix is equally specific, to scope it to the classes it was introduced
for, and we have not run it. A z-score calibration floor eliminated pair splitting
but raised unexpected top-1 detections from 35 to 90, most of them winning within
a millimetre, consistent with a floor too permissive to let organs vanish where
they are absent. The obvious response is to run the scoped variant. We stop
instead, for a reason about the evaluation set: the 180-vertebra set has already
absorbed the calibration fit, the bilateral-grouping change, the move to
per-vertebra scoring, these two variants and now their reruns, so further tests
against it would compound a multiple-comparisons exposure that is already
non-trivial. This says what our evaluation set can still support, not that
nothing remains to be found.

\subsection{Limitations and what they bound}

Several limitations bound what these results support. Patch supervision is
fixed at $14\times14$ by ViT-B/16 geometry, so the HD95 of 19.6\,px largely
reflects that grid rather than gross mislocalization, and raising input
resolution is not free, because doing so would change the geometry the
calibration and mask-formation path is built around. The equal-supervision probe
was trained for a fixed five epochs and had not converged, so the tie is if
anything generous to our method. The Phase~1 numbers are 2D per-slice metrics on
the five largest cross-sections of each organ in each volume. They therefore
describe organ interiors, and Appendix~\ref{app:extent} shows that accuracy falls
sharply toward the cranial and caudal ends of an organ, where cross-sections are
small. They are also more forgiving than 3D volumetric scoring, a caveat every
cross-paper comparison inherits. The anatomical descriptions were
used in training as well as evaluation, so the ablation cannot fully distinguish
``never necessary'' from ``already internalized'', and the ablation itself ran
on 300 test slices.

The downstream analysis measures distance from the whole involved vertebra, not
from the lesion, because the collection provides no lesion delineation, so it
tests whether the organ maps transfer and not how close a tumor is to an organ.
It uses 46 patients from a single collection, without external validation or a
clinical reference standard, the per-level analysis rests on 7--17 lesions per
level, and the pipeline was developed on the same cohort, so the results are
exploratory. Three corrections made on this cohort are reported in full. The calibration constants were first fitted on evaluation patients, then
on 35 patients of whom 26 were later evaluated per lesion, and are now
cross-fitted so that no patient is calibrated on its own scan. The vertebra masks
were mirrored front to back by an index-for-index placement of the SEG frames
until the revision, and every downstream number is recomputed with the
geometry-based placement. One error in the expected-organ table was corrected from
the literature before its effect was measured. One such error is reason to think
others may remain, particularly in the thoracolumbar region where the anatomy is
genuinely variable. The most useful next steps are an independent cohort with
lesion-level delineation, the per-organ-scoped negative variant, which addresses a
mechanism already diagnosed, and hardening bilateral prompts with
position-invariant cues, with the adversarial swap as a routine pre-deployment
check.

\subsection{Extending to 3D}
\label{sec:discussion:3d}

The method is 2D. Each axial slice is encoded independently, and in the
downstream analysis the per-slice maps are stacked, with competitive assignment
and connected components the only operations that see more than one slice.
Nothing enforces consistency between neighboring slices, so per-slice noise
appears as speckle and as organs that flicker in and out of adjacent slices. This is one route by which a phantom detection can persist through
assignment. Three extensions keep the patch-grid readout. The first is a 2.5D
input: the encoder already takes three input channels, and our pipeline fills them with three copies of one slice, and filling them with the slice and its two
neighbors gives every patch through-plane context with no change to the
architecture or to the $14\times14$ grid. It requires retraining, and a
through-plane spacing policy, since the neighbors of a 5\,mm slice and of a
1\,mm slice carry different context. The second regularizes the stacked
similarity volume before assignment, by smoothing along the through-plane axis
or with a 3D conditional random field over the eleven maps, trading some
sharpness at organ ends for consistency without retraining. The third is a
volumetric encoder with 3D patch tokens, though that would need a volumetric
vision--language backbone and full retraining. Any of these would also need
volumetric evaluation that includes the organ ends our slice protocol excludes,
and inter-slice consistency would itself become a reported metric, for example
the fraction of slice-to-slice transitions in which an organ appears or vanishes
away from its true extent.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We asked whether a text prompt can localize an organ in CT, and what part of the
prompt does the work. Making patch-level text--image alignment an explicit
training objective, against masks pooled to the $14\times14$ patch grid, turns
BioMedCLIP into a text-prompted organ localizer read straight off the forward
pass. It reaches 0.651 mIoU across eleven classes with validation-chosen
thresholds, on par with a linear probe given the same targets without text
(0.646). What the text adds is that the organ is selected by a written prompt,
with nothing between the prompt and the mask.

That property is what the rest of the paper uses. A controlled ablation over five
prompt formulations, varying only the text embedding and retraining nothing,
shows that the class name alone recovers 96.9\% of the full anatomical
description's mIoU, that position language without the organ name scores below
the name alone (0.561 vs.\ 0.607), and that an adversarial position swap costs up
to 56.8\% on the classes that depend on laterality while leaving uniquely
positioned organs untouched. Applied frozen to a different collection and
acquisition protocol, in an exploratory analysis, ranking organs by distance
from each involved vertebra places an anatomically expected organ at mean rank
1.82 against a chance of 2.91 over 180 involved vertebrae in 46 patients
($p<0.0001$, patient-clustered permutation test), with the residual errors
concentrated in \org{bladder} and the two femur classes.

Two conclusions generalize past this pipeline. Text supervision is carried by
class identity and by position language that refines it, not by descriptive
length, so anyone extending this approach should start with class names and
one-line captions. And when a text-prompted localizer fails it fails confidently,
segmenting the wrong structure cleanly rather than signaling uncertainty, which
makes an adversarial prompt swap a cheap pre-deployment check and prompt
validation a requirement rather than a courtesy.

The method remains bounded by the $14\times14$ patch grid, which accounts for most
of the HD95 of 19.6\,px and for the misses at organ ends, by the bladder and femur
failures that we diagnose but do not repair, and by single-cohort, exploratory
evaluation downstream on 46 patients with no lesion-level reference. The next
experiment follows from the diagnosis rather than from a search: scope hard
negatives to the classes they were introduced to correct, instead of subtracting
them from every organ. Beyond that, hardening bilateral prompts with
position-invariant morphological cues and validating on an independent cohort
with lesion-level delineation are what the method most needs before any clinical
claim.

\section{Anatomical basis for the expected-organ table}
\label{app:anatomy}

The question, fixed before the table was edited and before the validation was
re-scored, was whether the \code{EXPECTED} entries for T11 and T12 should include
the lungs. Changing the table and keeping whichever version improved the metric
would have made the evaluation circular. Oh et al.\
\cite{oh2009retrodiaphragmatic} reviewed 540 posteroanterior chest radiographs,
282 of which met inclusion criteria (155 men, 127 women, mean age
$40.7\pm8.4$ years); mean right posterior costophrenic sulcus depth was
$29.2\pm15.6$\,mm at an average spinal level of $13.5\pm0.6$, the lower half of
the L1 vertebral body and a full level below T12. Three further sources agree on
direction and none reported a left--right asymmetry, so both T11 and T12 include
\org{lung-left} and \org{lung-right}.

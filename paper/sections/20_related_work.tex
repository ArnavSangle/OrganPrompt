\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Vision--language pretraining and its biomedical adaptations}

CLIP established that predicting which caption belongs to which image, over 400
million web-collected pairs, yields representations strong enough for zero-shot
transfer across more than 30 vision benchmarks \cite{radford2021clip}. SigLIP
later replaced the softmax-normalized contrastive objective with a pairwise
sigmoid loss that needs no global view of the similarity matrix, which decouples
the loss from batch size and improves results at both small and very large
batches \cite{zhai2023siglip}.

Much of the subsequent progress has been attributed to data rather than
architecture. MetaCLIP argues explicitly that CLIP's principal ingredient is its
curation procedure, and recovers CLIP-level performance by balancing a raw pool
against metadata derived from CLIP's own concept list \cite{xu2024metaclip}.
DataComp makes the same point by construction, fixing the training code and
treating the dataset as the object of study across a 12.8-billion-pair candidate
pool \cite{gadre2023datacomp}. Both results motivate the biomedical variants,
where the binding constraint is domain-matched data.

Two such adaptations are directly relevant. PMC-CLIP assembles PMC-OA, 1.6M
image--caption pairs from PubMed Central's Open Access subset, most aligned at
subfigure and subcaption granularity \cite{lin2023pmcclip}. BiomedCLIP scales
this further with PMC-15M, 15 million figure--caption pairs drawn from 4.4
million articles, and adapts the text side to the domain: a PubMedBERT encoder
with a 30k domain-specific vocabulary in place of a general-domain tokenizer,
and a context length raised to 256 tokens to accommodate the length of PMC
captions \cite{zhang2025biomedclip}. That longer context is what makes
multi-sentence anatomical prompts expressible at all, and this work builds on
BiomedCLIP for that reason.

The limitation these models share is the one this work addresses. All of them
optimize alignment between a whole image and a whole caption at the level of a
pooled global embedding. Nothing in that objective requires any individual patch
token to be spatially discriminative, so per-patch similarity read off a
CLS-trained model carries little localization signal. Section~\ref{sec:results}
quantifies this: before fine-tuning, the pipeline reaches 0.003 mIoU.

\subsection{Dense and local alignment from vision--language models}

We are not the first to observe that global alignment is the obstacle, and the
work closest to ours attacks it in three distinguishable ways.

The first extracts dense correspondence from an already-trained model without
changing its weights. MaskCLIP shows that CLIP's value projection retains
substantially more spatial information than the pooled embedding suggests, and
recovers usable dense labels by reading it directly \cite{zhou2022maskclip}.
Subsequent work pushes this further for weakly supervised semantic segmentation
\cite{yang2025clipdense}, and a related line examines why CLIP's raw attention
localizes poorly and how its explainability can be repaired
\cite{li2024clipexplain}. Self-supervised vision transformers exhibit a similar
property, with DINO producing segmentation-like attention maps without any
textual supervision \cite{caron2021dino}. We take the same view of where the
signal lives, but we train the property in rather than recovering it, because
our downstream index requires organ maps that vanish where the organ is absent,
which is a calibration property rather than a localization one.

The second adapts the model to dense prediction with a task head. DenseCLIP
converts image-text matching into pixel-text matching and fine-tunes with a
dense decoder \cite{rao2022denseclip}, and CLIPSeg attaches a lightweight
transformer decoder that emits a segmentation from a text or image prompt
\cite{lueddecke2022clipseg}. Both produce better boundaries than we do. Both
also place a trained decoder between the prompt and the mask, which is precisely
the attribution problem we describe below.

The third is local alignment during pretraining, and GLoRIA is the closest
medical antecedent: it learns global and local representations jointly by
matching image sub-regions to individual words in radiology reports
\cite{huang2021gloria}. Our objective is the same in spirit. It differs in
supervision and in target: GLoRIA attends over report words to build
localized representations for label-efficient recognition, whereas we supervise
each of the 196 patch tokens directly against a downsampled organ mask, so that
the resulting per-patch similarity is itself the segmentation rather than an
intermediate representation.

\subsection{Class activation maps and post-hoc localization}

The established route from image-level labels to spatial maps is the CAM family.
CAM recovers localization from a classifier's final-layer activations
\cite{zhou2016cam}, and Grad-CAM generalizes this to arbitrary architectures by
weighting activations with backpropagated gradients \cite{selvaraju2017gradcam},
with later refinements such as Grad-CAM++ improving multi-instance localization
\cite{chattopadhay2018gradcampp}.
Both explain where a trained classifier looked.

That framing is a poor fit here, for a reason worth stating plainly rather than
asserting. A CAM explains a decision the network already makes. A contrastively
pretrained vision--language model makes no per-organ decision to explain; it
produces an embedding whose alignment with a text embedding is a similarity, not
a class logit. Applying gradient-based localization to it recovers almost
nothing, which is what the 0.003 starting point in Table~\ref{tab:progression}
measures. This work therefore trains the spatial property directly instead of
attempting to extract it post hoc, and the name reflects that: the prompt does
the localizing, and no activation map is involved.

\subsection{Text-driven medical image segmentation}

The closest methodological relative is MedCLIP-SAMv2, which shares both the
BiomedCLIP backbone and the premise that a text prompt can drive segmentation
\cite{koleilat2025medclipsamv2}, extending an earlier version of the same
framework \cite{koleilat2024medclipsam}. It differs in where the mask comes from:
saliency derived from the vision--language model is used to prompt SAM
\cite{kirillov2023sam}, which produces the final boundary, in a lineage that
includes MedSAM's adaptation of SAM to medical images \cite{ma2024medsam}.

Delegating mask formation to a second model is the stronger engineering choice
and generally yields better boundaries. It also changes what the pipeline can be
asked. When a refiner sits between the prompt and the output, a failure cannot
be attributed cleanly to the text, because the refiner may repair a bad
localization or corrupt a good one. Reading patch similarity directly, as here,
gives up boundary quality and gains attribution: the caption ablation in
\S\ref{sec:results:ablation} is only interpretable because nothing stands
between the prompt and the mask. We regard that trade as the substantive
difference between the two approaches, not the scores in
Table~\ref{tab:weaksup}, which are computed on different datasets and organ
sets.

\subsection{Weak supervision, stratified by annotation cost}

``Weakly supervised'' covers a wide range of supervision strength, and comparing
across that range without saying so is misleading. Point-supervised methods
receive one clicked pixel inside each structure; scribble-supervised methods
receive a stroke through it. Recent work in this tier reports substantial gains
over prior point- and scribble-supervised baselines on BTCV and CHAOS
\cite{jiang2024probabilistic}. Multi-organ abdominal CT benchmarks such as AMOS
\cite{ji2022amos} and WORD \cite{luo2022word} are the usual evaluation targets in
this tier, which is one reason no row of Table~\ref{tab:weaksup} shares a dataset
with our work.

Both forms of annotation answer ``where'' for each training image. Image-level
text supervision does not, which is the entire problem being solved here, so
these methods are context rather than baselines. The strictly comparable tier is
image-level supervision, where weakly supervised CAM-based lesion segmentation
in PET-CT reports a median Dice of 0.47 against 0.72 for a fully supervised
U-Net \cite{ronneberger2015unet} on the same task \cite{fruh2021petct}. That ratio, roughly two thirds of
supervised performance recovered from image-level labels alone, is the
expectation this work should be read against.

\subsection{Fully supervised reference}

TotalSegmentator provides both the dataset and the supervised ceiling. It trains
an nnU-Net \cite{isensee2021nnunet} on 1{,}204 CT examinations sampled from
routine clinical practice, covering 104 anatomical structures, and reports a
mean Dice of 0.943 on a test set spanning a wide range of pathology
\cite{wasserthal2023totalsegmentator}. Transformer-based architectures such as
TransUNet \cite{chen2021transunet} are a common alternative in this fully
supervised setting. The released dataset
\cite{wasserthal2023totalsegdataset} is what this work trains on, and the
published per-structure Dice is the reference against which
Table~\ref{tab:fullysup} is read, subject to the 2D/3D metric caveat stated
there.

\subsection{Positioning}

We validated the underlying idea on the OrganAMNIST subset of MedMNIST v2
\cite{yang2023medmnist} before moving to full-resolution CT, and we report
results here only on the latter.

This work sits in the image-level tier and differs from its nearest neighbours
in three respects. It trains patch-level text--image alignment as an explicit
objective instead of extracting localization post hoc from a classifier. It
reads the resulting similarity map directly, with no second-stage refiner, which
preserves the ability to attribute failures to the prompt. And it carries the
same frozen model into a downstream clinical question, the bone-metastasis
proximity index of \S\ref{sec:results:index}, on a dataset and acquisition
protocol the model never saw during training.
